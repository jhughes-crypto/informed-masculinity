## 2. The Kind Humor & “Fun Auntie” Pedagogy Playbook

### 2.1 Why kind humor works (the science behind the squirm)

**Benign Violation Theory.** Psychologists Peter McGraw and Caleb Warren (2010) showed that we laugh when something feels like a *violation* (it breaks a norm, feels risky or “wrong”) and, at the same moment, feels *benign* (safe, okay, not actually a threat). For a 9-year-old, an adult saying “scrotum” on a video call is a huge norm violation. Your calm, grinning, unbothered warmth is what makes it benign. The laugh that follows is the sound of a child's brain re-labeling the topic from *dangerous* to *safe*.

**Guided desensitization: exposure with warmth.** Modern exposure science (the *inhibitory learning* model; Craske et al., 2014) finds that fear shrinks fastest when people face the thing they dread and their predicted catastrophe **does not happen**. That's called *expectancy violation*. Every high-squirm word in this deck is a tiny, guided exposure trial:

1. **Prediction:** “If I hear or say this word, something terrible happens: I'll die of embarrassment, someone will laugh at me, my parent will freak out.”
2. **Exposure:** Auntie says the word clearly and lets the cringe breathe for a beat.
3. **Expectancy violation:** Nothing bad happens. Auntie *names* that out loud (“Anyone on fire? No? That's data.”).
4. **New learning:** “I can handle this topic.” Repeated across 13 slides, it adds up to confidence.

**Why it matters beyond the giggles.** Kids who can name body parts accurately and who expect calm reactions from adults are more likely to ask health questions, report symptoms, and speak up when something feels wrong. The humor isn't decoration. It's the delivery system for **Objective 3** (a trusted adult they'll *actually* go to).

**Direction of the punchline.** Kind humor always punches *sideways or up*: at the topic, at the awkwardness itself, at you, and gently at the grown-ups (who opted in). It **never** punches down at a child's body, timing, size, skin, smell, voice, or question.

### 2.2 The 5 Golden Rules of Fun Auntie Energy

**Rule 1: Laugh *with* the squirm, never *at* the kid.**
The joke is always “this topic is SO awkward,” never “*you* are awkward.” Aim laughs at the universal experience (“every grown-up you know went through the puppy-paws phase”), not at one person.
- ✅ “Oh, you felt that one. I saw an eyebrow. Lovely.” (Aimed at the room, not a named kid.)
- ❌ Calling out a named student's reaction, or joking about anyone's height, voice, pimples, or development.

**Rule 2: Name the elephant before it gets awkward.**
Announce the squirm *before* it arrives (“Next slide is the big one. Hold on to your blanket forts.”). When kids know discomfort is expected, it stops feeling like a sign that something is wrong.
- ✅ “Fair warning: I'm going to say words that make your face feel warm.”
- ❌ Racing through Slide 7 hoping nobody notices. They notice. Speed tells them it's shameful.

**Rule 3: Welcome cameras-off hiding.**
Hiding is how kids regulate. Treat it as participation, not withdrawal: a kid under a blanket who is still listening is *doing exposure*.
- ✅ “Oh, I can feel three cameras turning off right now. Iconic. Stay under that blanket; you can still hear me.”
- ❌ “Can everyone turn their cameras back on?” Never require faces.

**Rule 4: Tease the parents gently.**
Grown-ups are the safest target in the room, and when kids watch adults get lovingly roasted *and survive*, they learn that adults can handle this topic too. Keep it warm, inclusive, and brief, and never single out an individual parent.
- ✅ “Grown-ups, I see you doing the ‘I'm just checking my email’ face. Nice try.”
- ✅ “Parents, your entire job here is: ‘No big deal, laundry's over there.’”
- ❌ Anything that could embarrass a specific family or reference a family's private situation.

**Rule 5: Land the “worst fears didn't come true” moment out loud.**
Exposure only fully works when the brain *notices* the predicted disaster didn't happen. So say it explicitly, repeatedly, and finally as a celebration on Slide 13.
- ✅ Mini-landings: “Type 🔥 if you burst into flames, or ✅ if you survived… All checkmarks? LOOK AT YOU.” (Slide 7) · “Is anybody on fire? That's data.” (Slide 3)
- ✅ Big landing: “Nobody burst into flames. The internet still works. Your worst fears did not come true.” (Slide 13)

## 4. Live Google Meet Playbook: Handling Awkward Moments & Q&A

Every script below follows the same three-beat pattern: **(1) name it warmly, (2) normalize it, (3) redirect to the learning.** Keep each response under 20 seconds. Momentum is a form of safety.

### 4.1 Dead silence in chat

*What it means:* Kids are watching, not disengaged. Silence on a video call is usually fear of being first, not boredom.

> “Ahh, the sacred silence. I love it. That's the sound of twenty brains going ‘I am NOT typing first.’ Here's a secret: the chat is anonymous, and I've literally heard every question there is. Let me start you off. The number one question kids your age ask me is: *‘Is it normal if one testicle hangs lower than the other?’* [PAUSE] Yes! Totally normal. See? Somebody was wondering that, and now nobody has to ask it. Your turn. Even a single emoji counts as participation in my book.”

**Backup moves:**
- Switch to a zero-risk prompt: “Type a 1 if you've learned at least one new word today.” Then: “Type a 2 if your face got warm at least once.”
- Have your moderator post a pre-loaded seed question as if from the queue.
- Point to the PubertyBot: “If you'd rather ask a robot that will never, ever tell anyone, here's the link.”

**Seed questions to keep ready:** “Why does my voice crack?” · “Is it weird to get an erection when you're not thinking about anything?” · “How do I know if I need deodorant?” · “What if I start puberty later than my friends?” · “Can girls get gynecomastia?” (Answer: the term is used for males; everybody has some chest tissue.)

### 4.2 Nonstop giggling (unmuted or chat full of 😂😂😂)

*What it means:* The exposure is working. Giggling is how an 8–11-year-old nervous system lets off steam. Don't shut it down; give it a container.

> “Oh, we've got GIGGLES. Beautiful. That's your brain letting off steam like a teapot. Let's do it on purpose: everybody get ALL your giggles out in the next five seconds. Ready? Go! [Count down 5-4-3-2-1, laughing along.] Okay! Giggle tank emptied. And notice: we said the word, we laughed, and absolutely nothing bad happened. That's the whole point. Back to the science.”

**Backup moves:**
- If one unmuted mic keeps going: the moderator quietly mutes it; you never name the student.
- If it keeps returning: “The giggles keep coming back, which means you're paying attention. I'll take it.”

### 4.3 A kid types a joke or troll question

*What it means:* Usually anxiety wearing a costume. Underneath most troll questions is a real question the kid is embarrassed to ask.

**Step 1 (moderator):** Don't display or read crude or hurtful messages verbatim. Hide or delete if the platform allows.
**Step 2 (you):** Extract the real question and answer it seriously, with zero shame and zero shaming.

> “I got a question that was trying to be funny, and honestly, respect the effort. But there's a real question hiding inside it, so let me answer the real one: [restate respectfully, e.g., ‘Does everybody's body look the same down there?’]. Nope! Bodies come in lots of normal shapes and sizes, and they change over years. Thanks for asking, mystery comedian.”

**If it's targeting another person** (naming a classmate, mocking someone's body):
> “Ah, quick reminder of our Squirm Agreement: we laugh at the *topic*, never at a person. That's the one rule I'm serious about. Let's keep going.”

Then move on. Don't linger, lecture, or try to identify the sender on screen. If it continues, follow your organization's removal policy and follow up privately with the caregiver who registered that seat.

### 4.4 A kid asks “Am I normal?” (or “Is something wrong with me?”)

*What it means:* This is the most important question you'll get. Treat it with full warmth and no humor aimed at the question itself.

> “I'm really glad someone asked that, because I promise you, at least half the people on this call are wondering the exact same thing right now. Here's the honest answer: the range of normal in puberty is HUGE. Early, later, more hair, less hair, one side different from the other side: normal, normal, normal. And here's the best part: you don't have to figure it out alone. If something is worrying you about your body, that is exactly what your doctor is for, and exactly what your parent or caregiver wants to help with. You can also ask the PubertyBot privately, any time. Asking is the brave move, and you just made it.”

**Clinical guardrails to mention only if relevant:** signs before age 9, no signs by about 14, pain or swelling in the testicles (urgent: tell an adult right away), or a lump that's growing. Frame it as: “Doctors like to check on these, and usually everything is fine.”

### 4.5 A parent looks mortified on camera

*What it means:* The grown-up is doing their own exposure trial, and their kid may be watching their face for cues.

> “[Warmly, to the whole room, never naming anyone] Grown-ups, I see some of you doing the full-body cringe, and I want you to know: you're doing great. Truly. Your kid is watching how you handle this, and just by staying on the call, you're showing them this topic is safe. That's a huge deal. Also, the ‘pretending to look at something off-screen’ move? Very convincing. 10 out of 10.”

**Follow-up:** Add a one-line note in the post-session caregiver email (§7.3) acknowledging that it's normal for adults to feel awkward too, with conversation starters.

### 4.6 A student wants to opt out of a prompt

*What it means:* Autonomy is part of safety. Opting out is a valid choice, and respecting it builds trust for future sessions.

> “Totally fine! Every prompt today is an invitation, not an assignment. You can type, you can think it in your head, or you can just listen. Listening counts. Blanket-fort mode is fully supported.”

**Do:** Offer a lower-stakes version (“Just type a ✅ if you thought of an answer”).
**Don't:** Call on, count, or comment on who did or didn't respond.

### 4.7 Bonus scenarios

**A question that's beyond this module's scope** (e.g., about sex or reproduction details):
> “Great question, and it's one that deserves a full, honest answer. It's a bit beyond what we cover in this module, so it's a perfect one to ask your parent or caregiver tonight, or the PubertyBot. Grown-ups, I'm sending you some tips on how to answer this one.”

**A kid unmutes and blurts out a body word:**
> “Correct use of medical vocabulary! Five points. Mics back to mute, my friend; let's keep the words in the chat.”

**Tech failure mid-squirm-peak:**
> “Of course the internet broke during the genital slide. The universe has a sense of humor. Nobody move, and nobody bursts into flames. I'm back in 30 seconds.”

## 5. Chat Moderation & Safety Protocol

1. **Roles.** The instructor teaches; a co-host/moderator screens the anonymous chat, groups duplicate questions, mutes stray mics, and passes questions to the instructor in a private channel.
2. **Read-aloud rule.** Questions are restated in respectful, medically accurate language. Never read crude wording verbatim and never identify a sender.
3. **Disclosure response.** If any message suggests a child may be unsafe (someone touching their private parts, threats, self-harm), the moderator flags it to Dr. Hughes immediately and follows Colorado mandatory-reporting obligations and organizational policy. On the call, respond calmly and without detail: *“Thank you for telling me. That's really important, and I'm going to make sure you get help. A grown-up from our team will follow up.”* Never investigate or question on the live call.
4. **Out-of-scope medical concerns.** Don't diagnose. Redirect: “That's a great one for your doctor.” Urgent red flag: sudden testicular pain or swelling means **tell an adult and get seen right away** (possible testicular torsion).
5. **Post-session.** Moderator exports the questions (no identifiers) for curriculum improvement and the PubertyBot FAQ.
